Classroom kit — watch a bill become law

A real bill being drafted in public, staged as a lesson.

This kit teaches how a bill moves — it does not ask anyone to support this one. The draft's own open questions are part of the lesson.

The bill needs a legislator willing to carry it. That has not happened — and it may never. This is the stage most bills never pass.

Committee

If filed, it goes to a housing committee: hearings, testimony, amendments, a fiscal note. Most bills stop here.

Floor → Law

House and Senate votes, a signature — then the part the draft already plans for: a mandatory year-3 report and a sunset, so the law has to prove itself to survive.

Lesson outline — one class period

  1. Open (5 min). Read the the lead story aloud or on screens. Ask: what problem is this draft actually trying to solve?
  2. Read the real text (10 min). Open the live draft. Each student picks one locked clause and puts it in their own words.
  3. Trace a decision (15 min). On the version history, find where a clause changed — the 10/23 amendments are the clearest example. What changed? Why? Was the reason a person, a number, or an argument?
  4. Map the journey (10 min). Place the bill on the stage list above. What has to happen next, and who decides whether it does?
  5. Discuss (10 min). Pick from the questions below.

Extension. Pick one figure on The numbers, follow it to its source, and report back whether you think it earned its "verified" mark.

Glossary

Bill
A proposed law, written down. It is nothing more until a legislature passes it.
Sponsor (carrier)
The legislator who introduces a bill and argues for it.
Revisor's office
The nonpartisan office that turns a working draft into official bill language — numbering, cross-references, statute form.
Committee
The small group of legislators that hears a bill first. Most bills end here.
Fiscal note
The official estimate of what a bill would cost the state, prepared for the committee.
Actuarial
The math that prices risk — what an insurance pool must charge to pay its claims.
Escrow
Money held by a neutral third party until the conditions for releasing it are met.
Premium
The price of insurance coverage.
Subrogation
When an insurer pays a claim and then collects the money back from the person at fault. This bill bans it — a paid claim ends the matter.
Adjudication
A formal decision made by a neutral body — here, an administrative one rather than a courtroom.
Title (of a bill)
A major section grouping related rules — this draft has four.
Pilot
A small first run of a program, used to gather real numbers before committing statewide.
Sunset clause
A built-in end date: the law expires unless the legislature renews it.
Registry
A public list — here, aggregate claims data anyone can check.

Discussion questions

  • Who should hold a damage deposit — the landlord, the tenant, or a neutral third party? What could go wrong each way?
  • A safety net has to be paid for. Who should pay, and why? What does each of the bill's three funding legs ask of the people paying it?
  • What should happen to someone who files a false claim — whichever side of the lease they are on? The bill makes the penalty symmetric; is that the right call?
  • The authors list their own open questions and what the bill might never fix. Does showing doubt make a proposal more trustworthy, or less?
  • What would you personally need to know before you would vote for this — and is that information public on this site?

A note for teachers

Everything a student sees here is dated and sourced, and the whole record — every edit, every decision, every correction — is public in the site's version history. If the bill moves, this page moves with it; if it stalls, the record of how far it got stays readable either way.
