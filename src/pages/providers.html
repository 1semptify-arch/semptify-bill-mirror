Semptify

For housing providers

What the bill asks of you — and what it gives back.

The draft is designed to be fair and square with everybody: providers keep the right to make money, and a clean record is worth something. This page is the honest version — including the part that costs you.

What it asks of you

  • Deposits move to bonded escrow. At lease signing, the security deposit goes into program escrow and stays there for the tenancy. You never hold it, itemize it, or return it — an independent adjudicator settles it at move-out. The tenant keeps the statutory 1% interest. The float above that 1% — about 3% on escrowed deposits — is the funding leg, and it is the real cost to you: the float you hold today is the float you would no longer hold.
  • A matched share, priced like insurance. A community-rated base plus a bounded experience surcharge — in the current working model, about $6 per unit per month at $1,300 rent. Claims history prices it: a rougher book pays more, a clean book pays less and earns money back.
  • One documentation standard. Plain rules for how problems get documented — the same standard for a four-unit owner and a four-hundred-unit operator. How to scale that fairly is one of the draft's open questions, and it says so.

What it gives back

  • Rent still arrives during verified hardship. When a covered tenant hits a verified crisis — job loss, medical — the fund pays the covered rent. The claim ends the matter: no subrogation against the tenant, and no eviction filed to protect your income.
  • Priced below one eviction. A single eviction filing costs a provider an estimated $5,600–$7,400 once lost rent, turnover, and legal time are counted (Harris County i4J model). The modeled matched share is a small fraction of that.
  • A clean book earns money back. Clean-book years earn a no-claim dividend declared from pool surplus — a mutual model, not a guaranteed payment. The same logic gives renters a claims-free dividend.
  • A good record defends you. The documentation standard that catches a manufactured claim is the same one that clears you when a claim isn't true. Fabricated claims carry a surcharge plus referral — on either side of the lease.
  • Deposits stop being your fight. Move-out disputes go to independent adjudication with a tenant counter-claim lane — no more small-claims deposit battles, and no more being the one holding the money both sides are arguing over.

Fair questions, answered plainly

"Is this just a tax with a nicer name?"

It is structured as insurance, not a tax: a matched share buys a real payout (covered rent during hardship, deposit adjudication), and clean books earn a no-claim dividend back from surplus. Whether the price is fair is exactly what the open model is for — the math is published, not asserted.

"Does a problem tenant just get covered?"

No. Hardship triggers are verified, coverage is capped per lease, claims history prices the renewal, and cooperation with a repayment plan on forward rent is a condition of continued coverage. Tenant-side abuse controls are designed to be as real as the provider-side penalties — that symmetry is a locked design call, not a talking point.

"Who decides claims?"

An independent adjudication body on an administrative-law model — not the landlord, and not the carrier administering the pool. There is a tenant counter-claim lane through the same body, and an administrative appeal path whose review standard is still an open drafting question.

"I'm a small operator — does this crush me?"

Honest answer: that's one of the open questions. The draft's own goal is one standard scaled so a four-unit owner isn't carrying paperwork built for a four-hundred-unit operator. That scaling rule is not finished — it is exactly the kind of input the draft is public to collect.

Where it stands

A working draft — not filed, not before any legislature, and it may never become law. The questions above are the ones the draft itself is asking; if you carry one we haven't answered, the public board is where it goes.
